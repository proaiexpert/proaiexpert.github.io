# How to Evaluate a Website Proposal Before You Sign

Two website proposals can list the same number of pages and describe different projects.

One “five-page website” may cover design and development only. Another may also include copy, mobile behavior, forms, redirects, analytics, testing, launch, and account handoff.

Until the scopes are normalized, comparing the totals tells you very little.

> Which proposal leaves fewer important responsibilities, dependencies, and completion standards undefined?

This guide gives U.S. service-business owners a practical system for reviewing a website proposal before signing.

> This is general business information, not legal advice about a specific proposal or contract.

## Reconcile what was sold with what will be signed

A website project may involve several documents:

- a **proposal** describing the approach, outcome, and price;
- a **statement of work, or SOW**, defining deliverables, responsibilities, timing, and completion;
- a **contract or agreement** governing the legal relationship;
- an **estimate or quote**, whose effect depends on its wording;
- attachments covering specifications, pricing, licenses, or change orders.

The names vary. The important question is whether the documents describe the same project.

If the sales process promised:

- strategy;
- copywriting;
- search setup;
- a second language;
- integrations;
- analytics;
- post-launch support;

but the SOW includes only “design and development,” ask for the important sales promises to be included in the SOW, contract, or a clearly incorporated attachment before signing.

Do not treat an oral promise as defined operational scope until its written status is clear. Whether a particular proposal is legally binding depends on its text and applicable law; significant contract questions should be reviewed by a qualified U.S. attorney.

For each important promise, record where it appears: the document name, section or page, and current version. A note that something was “discussed on the call” is not the same as a defined written scope.

Evaluate each item across separate fields.

### Scope

- **INCLUDED**
- **EXCLUDED**
- **UNRESOLVED**

### Responsible party

- **CLIENT**
- **PROVIDER**
- **SHARED**

### External dependency

- **NONE**
- **THIRD PARTY**

### Result and acceptance

Record what must be delivered, how completion will be tested, and what evidence will confirm acceptance.

### Document source

Record the proposal, SOW, contract, or attachment—and the relevant section, page, and version.

One item may be included, assigned to the provider, dependent on a third-party service, and fully defined at the same time.

Use **NOT APPLICABLE** when a field does not apply, especially after an item has been explicitly excluded.

An explicit exclusion is better than a hidden assumption.

“Copywriting is not included; the client supplies approved copy before design” is clear.

“Content will be discussed later” is a risk.

## Confirm the business objective

A weak scope may promise a “modern, professional, high-converting website” without defining what the visitor should understand or do.

The written project should identify the primary audience, priority services, service area, customer decision, next step, important exclusions, and facts requiring approval.

The proposal does not need to contain a complete strategy before discovery. It should show whether strategy is a defined phase or outside the engagement.

## Define the actual scope

Page count is only one dimension.

Review five groups:

1. **Pages and content:** routes, copy, images, blog or resource content, language versions.
2. **Functions:** forms, notifications, calendars, CRM, payments, and other integrations.
3. **Migration:** current content, files, data, and old-URL redirects.
4. **Setup and verification:** analytics, Search Console, sitemap, robots, testing, and accessibility review.
5. **Launch and handoff:** deployment, training, credentials, documentation, and support transition.

Also clarify whether the engagement includes privacy notices and other pages or disclosures that may apply based on the site’s data practices, services, audience, and jurisdiction.

Not every project needs every item. The goal is a visible boundary.

A third-party dependency does not replace project ownership. The proposal should still identify who coordinates the third party, provides access, approves fees, and verifies the result.

A provider may draft copy while the business verifies services, geography, pricing language, licenses, and operational limitations. Sensitive professional claims may require qualified review.

## Separate ownership from practical control

“You own the website” is too broad. A website combines legal rights, licensed components, accounts, billing relationships, and administrative access.

### Domain

Confirm:

- who is listed as the registrant;
- whose registrar account contains the domain;
- who receives renewal notices;
- who controls DNS;
- what happens if the provider relationship ends.

ICANN describes the registrant as the person or entity registering the domain through a registrar relationship. See [ICANN’s information for domain registrants](https://www.icann.org/registrants).

### Hosting and deployment

Confirm:

- who owns or controls the hosting account;
- who pays recurring fees;
- who can publish or roll back a deployment;
- whether the site can be moved;
- what happens when a support plan ends.

### CMS and connected accounts

Check owner or admin access for:

- the content-management system;
- analytics;
- Search Console;
- Tag Manager;
- advertising pixels, when applicable;
- form, calendar, CRM, and email services.

The proposal should explain how the business retains or receives access to its data and operational accounts if a contractor account is removed or the relationship ends.

### Content, design, and code

Separate:

- client-provided materials;
- provider-created copy and design;
- custom code;
- stock images;
- fonts;
- themes and plugins;
- APIs and software licenses;
- AI-assisted assets;
- source files or repository access.

Payment does not automatically transfer every copyright interest in every protected project element.

Under U.S. copyright law, copyright initially vests in the author except where the law provides otherwise, including qualifying works made for hire. Copyright ownership may later be transferred through a valid transfer. A transfer of copyright ownership, other than by operation of law, generally requires a written document signed by the owner of the rights being transferred or an authorized agent. Work-made-for-hire treatment is limited. See the U.S. Copyright Office materials on [copyright ownership and transfer](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html) and [work made for hire](https://www.copyright.gov/register/se-hire.html).

The signed documents should explain:

- what is assigned;
- what is licensed;
- what remains provider-owned;
- what belongs to a third party;
- what the client may modify or move;
- whether source files or a repository are included.

Material intellectual-property questions should be reviewed by a qualified U.S. attorney.

## Make technology and dependencies visible

The platform matters because of how it is owned, maintained, paid for, and transferred—not because one product is universally better.

Ask:

- why the approach fits the business;
- how the platform is owned, maintained, and paid for;
- which fees recur;
- what depends on plugins, APIs, or external services;
- whether content and data can be exported;
- what happens if an external service changes.

“Calendar integration included” is incomplete. A clearer scope identifies:

- the calendar product;
- where the integration appears;
- which account owns it;
- what data moves between systems;
- what confirmation the customer receives;
- what is tested;
- which third-party fees are excluded;
- what fallback exists if the service fails.

## Define “done” before launch

Launch is an event. Acceptance is a decision that the delivered work meets the agreed scope.

Possible acceptance areas include:

- agreed pages and content;
- mobile behavior;
- navigation, links, forms, and notifications;
- language routes;
- page titles, descriptions, canonical, and `hreflang` when applicable;
- redirects;
- sitemap, robots, analytics, and Search Console;
- accessibility and performance review;
- credentials, documentation, backup, or rollback;
- owner sign-off.

For important checks, define the evidence rather than writing only “tested” or “included.”

| Check | Responsible party | Method and evidence | Pass condition | Blocks launch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Form and notifications |  |  |  |  |
| Mobile behavior |  |  |  |  |
| Redirects |  |  |  |  |
| Analytics |  |  |  |  |
| Accessibility review |  |  |  |  |
| Credential handoff |  |  |  |  |

A small project still needs an agreed definition of completion.

For accessibility, W3C recommends evaluation early and throughout development, when issues are easier to correct. W3C also states that no automated tool alone can determine whether a site is accessible; knowledgeable human evaluation is required. See [Evaluating Web Accessibility](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/).

One Lighthouse score should not be the entire definition of quality. The proposal should identify the checks that matter for the project and which issues block launch.

## Separate revisions, change requests, defects, and maintenance

These terms should not be interchangeable.

### Revision

A change within an agreed deliverable before approval.

### Change request

New or changed scope.

### Defect

Delivered behavior that does not meet the agreed result or acceptance criteria.

### Maintenance

Post-launch work such as:

- updates;
- backups;
- security;
- content changes;
- integration checks;
- platform care.

The documents should define revision rounds, feedback and approval, change pricing, defect correction, maintenance, support end dates, and responsibility for urgent failures.

The problem is not a short support period; it is an undefined one.

## Review evidence, not just portfolio aesthetics

A polished screenshot proves presentation, not process or business performance. Identify what you are reviewing:

- a live website;
- a screenshot;
- a Concept Project;
- a template;
- a redesign mockup;
- a case study;
- a testimonial;
- a verified metric.

Ask whether the site is live, what the provider actually delivered, whether mobile behavior and forms can be inspected, whether the case separates outputs from outcomes, and whether any metrics are dated and sourced.

A Concept Project can demonstrate strategy, structure, and design direction, but not a client result.

## Use warning signals without rigid rules

Material warnings include:

- no written scope;
- guaranteed rankings or leads;
- unclear account control;
- undisclosed recurring costs;
- no acceptance process;
- live work claimed with screenshots only;
- pressure to sign while important questions remain unresolved;
- no owner for content and factual review.

These items require context rather than automatic rejection:

- a large deposit;
- a small team;
- template use;
- a proprietary platform;
- a monthly plan;
- remote work;
- AI-assisted production;
- no public pricing.

Ask:

1. What risk does this model create?
2. Is that risk disclosed and acceptable to the business?

A proprietary platform may be useful, but the proposal should explain access, export, migration, and what happens when payment ends.

For each important promise, record the document, section or page, and current version.

Assign a risk color only after reviewing scope, responsibility, client input, external dependencies, acceptance evidence, and document source:

- **green** — the item is defined or explicitly excluded;
- **yellow** — clarification is still required, but the exposure can be evaluated;
- **red** — an important issue remains unresolved and may affect cost, launch, control, or the ability to change providers.

Before signing, handle a red item in one of three ways:

1. clarify it and add the resolution to the documents;
2. explicitly exclude it from the engagement;
3. knowingly accept the risk and its operational or financial consequences.

The importance of each item depends on the project. A lower-cost proposal can fit an intentionally narrow scope; a higher-cost proposal is not automatically complete.

## A practical decision sequence

Before signing:

1. Reconcile the sales discussion, proposal, SOW, contract, and attachments.
2. Define scope for each important item.
3. Assign responsibility and client inputs.
4. Record external dependencies.
5. Map legal rights and practical control.
6. Define acceptance evidence.
7. Record the document source and version.
8. Separate revisions, new scope, defects, and maintenance.
9. Resolve, exclude, or knowingly accept red risks.
10. Compare price last.

The goal is to prevent important risk from hiding inside an assumption.
