# How to Evaluate a Website Proposal Before You Sign

**Due Diligence Guide** | Evaluating IT Contracts for Service Businesses

> **Executive Summary**
> Two website proposals can quote a "five-page website" and describe entirely different business outcomes. The risk of expensive rebuilds and hidden fees arises when responsibilities, third-party dependencies, account ownership, and acceptance criteria remain undefined before signing. You must evaluate a proposal based on the volume of resolved risks, not just the page count or the bottom-line price.

The U.S. web development market is saturated with options, from solo freelancers to enterprise agencies. When a business owner receives multiple website proposals, the immediate instinct is to compare the total price.

The danger lies in the scope. In one proposal, a "website" might mean design and basic development only. In another, it includes copywriting, CRM integration, mobile optimization, SEO redirects, accessibility testing, and the formal transfer of intellectual property rights.

Until the scope across all proposals is normalized, comparing the totals tells you very little. The safest way to begin vendor selection is not by asking, "Who is cheapest?" but rather, **"Which proposal leaves the fewest important business processes as unwritten assumptions?"**

Below is the Proposal Risk Ledger system, designed to help U.S. service business owners evaluate digital proposals thoroughly before signing.

*(This material provides general business information and does not substitute for legal review of a specific contract by a qualified U.S. attorney).*

## 1. The Illusion of Page Count

<aside class="scenario-card">
**Scenario: Same Scope, Different Deliverables**

Proposal **A** and Proposal **B** both quote a 5-page website.

*   **Agency A:** Provides layout design and development. The client must supply all final text. The site is launched on the agency's proprietary server.
*   **Agency B:** Conducts interviews to draft the copy, configures contact forms to route directly into the client's CRM, maps old URLs (301 redirects) to preserve SEO traffic, and hands over full administrative hosting rights.

Proposal **B** carries a higher initial cost. However, choosing Proposal **A** means the client will later pay separately for a copywriter, lose organic traffic due to broken links, and remain dependent on the agency's infrastructure.
</aside>

Page count is a superficial metric. Due diligence requires evaluating the infrastructure, rights, and processes that support those pages.

## 2. The Proposal Risk Ledger

A professional proposal, Statement of Work (SOW), or estimate can be broken down to expose "blind spots." For every major component (e.g., content, forms, analytics, launch), apply the following evaluation criteria:

1.  **Scope:** Is the item INCLUDED, EXCLUDED, or UNRESOLVED? *(An "unresolved" item is a future invoice).*
2.  **Responsibility:** Who executes the work: the Provider, the Client, or a Third Party?
3.  **External Dependency:** Does this feature require a paid third-party service (e.g., CRM subscription, premium API)?
4.  **Acceptance (Definition of Done):** What exact evidence will confirm this task is complete?
5.  **Documentation Traceability:** In which document, and on what page, is this promise written? ("Discussed on the call" is not a valid scope).

**Key Areas for the Ledger:**
*   **Content:** Who writes the copy, sources imagery, and handles translations? Projects stall most frequently because content ownership was never explicitly assigned.
*   **Integrations:** "Calendar integration included" is too vague. Who owns the calendar account? Who pays the monthly fee? How is data routed?
*   **Technical SEO:** Are 301 redirects from the old site to the new site included? Without them, launching a new site can devastate existing search visibility.

## 3. The Ownership Audit: Control vs. Legal Rights

A salesperson promising "You own the website" is legally imprecise. A website is not a single physical object; it is an assembly of domain registrations, hosting accounts, third-party software licenses, custom code, and copyrights. 

<aside class="authority-block">
**Domains and ICANN**
The true "owner" of a domain is the person or entity listed as the *Registrant* in the registrar’s database. If an agency registers the domain under its own name, you are effectively renting it. Ensure you have administrative access to the registrar and control the DNS records directly. (Reference: [ICANN for Registrants](https://www.icann.org/registrants)).
</aside>

<aside class="authority-block">
**Copyrights and the U.S. Copyright Office**
Under U.S. copyright law, copyright initially vests in the author (the creator) of the work. Paying an invoice for web development does not automatically transfer all intellectual property rights to the client. A transfer of copyright ownership generally requires a written document signed by the owner of the rights being transferred. The "work made for hire" doctrine applies only in specific statutory circumstances. Licenses for third-party fonts or plugins remain with their respective creators. (Reference: [U.S. Copyright Office Circular 92](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html)).
</aside>

**Establish a Business Control Map:**
Before work begins, document who holds *Admin access* and who is the *Billing owner* for: Domain, Hosting, CMS (e.g., WordPress), Analytics, Advertising accounts, and the Code Repository.

## 4. Definition of Done: Acceptance Criteria

Launch is an event. Acceptance is the formal confirmation that the delivered work satisfies the contracted scope. Do not sign an agreement that lacks a clear acceptance process.

**Checks to Require Before Launch:**
*   Mobile behavior verification (forms and navigation must be fully functional, not just "visible").
*   End-to-end testing of all contact forms and notification routing.
*   Confirmation of foundational analytics (e.g., Google Analytics 4, Search Console) setup.
*   Accessibility Review. *While automated tools (like Lighthouse) are helpful, the W3C explicitly states that knowledgeable human evaluation is required to determine true accessibility conformance.*
*   Secure handoff of all administrative credentials to the client.

---

## 5. The Red-Flag Decision Gate

If a proposal contains the following issues, halt the process until they are resolved in writing:

*   [ ] **No written SOW:** The agency provides only a high-level presentation and an invoice, with no detailed Statement of Work.
*   [ ] **Guaranteed Rankings:** The provider guarantees a #1 spot on Google or a specific volume of leads.
*   [ ] **Withheld Access:** The agency refuses to grant the client administrative access to the hosting environment or domain registrar.
*   [ ] **Vague Acceptance:** There is no defined process outlining how the client reviews and approves the final deliverable.
*   [ ] **Blurring Revisions vs. Maintenance:** The contract does not distinguish between fixing pre-launch defects and post-launch paid maintenance.

For any red flag identified before signing, you must do one of three things: clarify it in the SOW, explicitly exclude it, or knowingly accept the risk and its future financial consequences.
