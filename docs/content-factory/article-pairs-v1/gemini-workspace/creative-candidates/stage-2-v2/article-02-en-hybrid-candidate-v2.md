# How to Evaluate a Website Proposal Before You Sign

**Due Diligence Guide** | Evaluating IT Contracts for Service Businesses

> **Executive Summary**
> Two website proposals can quote a "five-page website" and describe entirely different projects. The risk of expensive rebuilds arises when responsibilities, third-party dependencies, account ownership, licenses, and acceptance criteria remain undefined before signing. You must evaluate a proposal based on the volume of resolved risks, not just the page count or the bottom-line price.

The U.S. web development market is saturated. When a business owner receives multiple website proposals, the immediate instinct is to compare the total price.

The danger lies in the scope. In one proposal, a "five-page website" might mean design and basic development only. In another, it includes copywriting, mobile behavior, forms, old-URL redirects, analytics, testing, launch, and formal account handoff.

Until the scope across all proposals is normalized, comparing the totals tells you very little. The safest way to begin vendor selection is not by asking, "Who is cheapest?" but rather, **"Which proposal leaves the fewest important responsibilities, dependencies, and completion standards undefined?"**

Below is the Proposal Risk Ledger system, designed to help U.S. service business owners evaluate digital proposals thoroughly before signing.

*(This guide provides general business information, not legal advice about a specific proposal or contract).*

---

## 1. Sales-to-Signature Reconciliation

A website project may involve several documents:
*   **Proposal:** Describes the approach, outcome, and price.
*   **Statement of Work (SOW):** Defines deliverables, responsibilities, timing, and completion.
*   **Contract or Agreement:** Governs the legal relationship.
*   **Estimate or Quote:** Preliminary or fixed cost assessment.

**The Rule:** Reconcile what was sold with what will be signed. If the sales process promised strategy, copywriting, search setup, or a second language, but the SOW includes only "design and development," ask for those promises to be included in the SOW or contract before signing. 
Do not treat an oral discussion as defined operational scope until its written status is clear. Record the document name, section, and version for every important promise. Whether a proposal is legally binding depends on its text and applicable law; significant contract questions should be reviewed by a qualified U.S. attorney.

---

## 2. The Proposal Risk Ledger

A professional proposal can be broken down to expose blind spots. For every major component (e.g., service copy, forms, redirects, analytics, deployment), apply the following evaluation criteria:

1.  **Scope:** Is the item INCLUDED, EXCLUDED, or UNRESOLVED? *(An explicit exclusion is better than a hidden assumption. An "unresolved" item is an exposure).*
2.  **Responsible Party:** Who executes the work: the CLIENT, the PROVIDER, or is it SHARED?
3.  **External Dependency:** Does this feature require a THIRD PARTY service?
4.  **Acceptance Evidence:** What exact evidence will confirm this task is complete?
5.  **Document Source:** Where is this explicitly documented?

Record `NOT APPLICABLE` when a field does not apply, especially after an item has been explicitly excluded. The goal is a visible boundary.

---

## 3. Responsibility and Client Input Matrix

Website projects often stall because no one owns a material, approval, or decision. Use the Ledger to define who provides and approves positioning, service facts, copy, translations, photography, privacy/legal review, and final sign-off.

A provider may draft copy, but the client must verify services, geography, pricing language, and operational limitations. Sensitive professional claims may require qualified review. A third-party dependency (like an integration) does not replace project ownership; the proposal must identify who coordinates the third party, provides access, and pays the fees.

---

## 4. The Ownership Audit: Rights and Practical Control

"You own the website" is too broad. A website combines legal rights, licensed components, accounts, billing relationships, and administrative access. Build a Business Control Map to separate ownership from practical control.

<aside class="authority-block">
**Domains and Hosting (ICANN)**
ICANN describes the registrant as the person or entity registering the domain through a registrar relationship. Confirm who is listed as the registrant, whose registrar account contains the domain, who receives renewal notices, who controls DNS, who pays hosting fees, and what happens if the provider relationship ends. *(Source: [ICANN information for domain registrants](https://www.icann.org/registrants))*
</aside>

<aside class="authority-block">
**Content, Design, and Code (U.S. Copyright Office)**
Payment does not automatically transfer every copyright interest in every protected project element. Under U.S. copyright law, copyright initially vests in the author except where the law provides otherwise (including qualifying works made for hire). A transfer of copyright ownership generally requires a written document signed by the owner of the rights being transferred. Licenses for third-party fonts, themes, or APIs remain governed by their creators. *(Sources: [U.S. Copyright Office - Ownership and transfer](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html) and [Work made for hire](https://www.copyright.gov/register/se-hire.html))*
</aside>

Ensure the signed documents explain what is assigned, what is licensed, what remains provider-owned, and whether source files or repository access are included. Material IP questions should be reviewed by an attorney.

---

## 5. Technology and Dependencies

The platform matters because of how it is owned, maintained, paid for, and transferred. Ask which fees recur, what depends on plugins or APIs, whether content can be exported, and what happens if an external service fails.
"Calendar integration included" is incomplete. A clear scope identifies the calendar product, the account owner, the data passed, who pays the fee, and how it is tested.

---

## 6. Definition of Done: Acceptance Evidence

Launch is an event. Acceptance is a decision that the delivered work meets the agreed scope. Define the evidence for checks like mobile behavior, forms, redirects, and analytics.

**Accessibility Limitation:**
W3C recommends evaluation early and throughout development. However, W3C states that no automated tool alone can determine whether a site is accessible; knowledgeable human evaluation is required. One Lighthouse score should not be the entire definition of quality. *(Source: [W3C Evaluating Web Accessibility](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/))*

---

## 7. Revisions, Change Requests, Defects, and Maintenance

These terms are not interchangeable. Clarify them before starting:
*   **Revision:** A change within an agreed deliverable before approval.
*   **Change request:** New or changed scope.
*   **Defect:** Delivered behavior that does not meet the agreed result or acceptance criteria.
*   **Maintenance:** Post-launch work (updates, backups, security).

The documents should define revision rounds, how new scope is priced, and what happens when support ends. The problem is not a short support period; it is an undefined one.

---

## 8. Warning Signals and Context

Evaluate evidence, not just aesthetics. A screenshot proves presentation, not process or business performance. Ask if the site is live and if mobile behavior can be inspected. 

**Material warnings to resolve before signing:**
*   No written scope.
*   Guaranteed rankings or leads.
*   Unclear account control or undisclosed recurring costs.
*   No acceptance process.
*   Pressure to sign while important questions remain unresolved.

Some models (templates, proprietary platforms, monthly plans, AI-assisted production) are not automatic red flags. Ask: What risk does this model create, and is it disclosed and acceptable to the business?

---

## A Practical Decision Sequence

Compare normalized proposals. Before signing:

1.  Reconcile the sales discussion, proposal, SOW, contract, and attachments.
2.  Define scope (Included/Excluded) for each important item.
3.  Assign responsibility and client inputs.
4.  Record external dependencies and recurring costs.
5.  Map legal rights and practical account control.
6.  Define acceptance evidence.
7.  Record the document source and version for every promise.
8.  Separate revisions, new scope, defects, and maintenance.
9.  Resolve, explicitly exclude, or knowingly accept unresolved red risks.
10. Compare price last.

The goal is to prevent important risk from hiding inside an assumption.
