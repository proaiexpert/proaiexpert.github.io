How to Estimate Business Website Cost: A 24/36-Month TCO Worksheet

Estimate 24- or 36-month website ownership cost with a fill-in comparison of real vendor quotes, recurring fees, internal labor and transfer obligations.

18 min read2026-04-05Updated 2026-10-17
BUDGET RULE

Price a defined set of deliverables, dependencies and operating obligations—not an unexplained page count.

COMPARISON RULE

Normalize two proposals against the same acceptance criteria, ownership rights and post-launch horizon.

DECISION

Choose the smallest scope that can reliably support the real business process. Custom work is not automatically the better investment.

Two service businesses can each request an eight-page website and receive justifiably different proposals. One has approved copy, clear services, a simple contact route and no migration. The other needs positioning, bilingual service pages, booking integration, content migration, accessibility validation and an accountable handover. The pages are not the unit of work. The commitments behind them are.

Answer first: there is no defensible universal dollar answer to “how much does a business website cost?” A usable estimate requires written scope, comparable vendor quotations, verified recurring obligations and internal work at known company rates. This article offers a fillable 24/36-month total cost of ownership (TCO) worksheet for Proposal A and B, supported by the original ProAI Expert Website Investment Scope Model—not a database of U.S. agency prices or a prediction of ROI.

It is written for an owner of a U.S. service business deciding what to commission. The guide to evaluating a specific vendor proposal addresses accountability during selection. The guide to diagnosing lost enquiries investigates a live site's commercial performance. Here the narrower question is: what work should be purchased, paid for and owned?

How to estimate the cost of a business website

Cost depends on scope, the state of existing assets, the amount of uncertainty the supplier must resolve, the people responsible for decisions, integration and compliance requirements, the level of verification, and what happens after launch. Page count is a useful inventory item; it is a poor proxy for research, content accuracy, migration risk or operational integration. More pages can sometimes be cheaper than one highly integrated experience.

Start from the business's actual requirements: priority services and locations, purchase or enquiry process, language needs, sources of evidence, internal approvers, security and privacy duties, and acceptable maintenance workload. Make a supplier separate included, excluded and conditional work. A conditional item should state its trigger, decision owner and pricing mechanism. An apparent fixed quote that leaves essential decisions “to be discussed” carries unpriced risk.

What this article does not claim: No market-average dollar rates, guaranteed return on investment, universal per-page rate or measured client results are asserted here. A specific price needs a dated quote with an agreed scope, location, currency, assumptions and acceptance conditions.

The Website Investment Scope Model

The model has four linked ledgers. Scope records deliverables and evidence of completion. Dependency records assets, access, approvals and external interfaces needed to deliver. Lifecycle records recurring costs, operational work and change requests. Exit records what the client can transfer or operate if the relationship ends. A proposal is comparison-ready only when those four ledgers can be inspected together.

  1. Specify the outcome without promising a metric. For example: publish accurate service and coverage information and deliver a tested enquiry to the designated intake team.
  2. Name acceptance artifacts. Navigation map, approved content inventory, responsive screens, accessible controls, test logs and administrator access are more precise than “premium design.”
  3. Expose dependencies. Who supplies photos, translations, credentials, CRM fields, redirects, legal text and approvals? What happens when they are late?
  4. Put operations and exit on the same page. Include hosting, licensed components, backups, security updates, service response, access control and data/code handover.
  5. Compare proposals only after normalization. Add excluded but required items as separate rows; do not assume an omitted task is free or included.

The model prevents a false choice between “cheap” and “premium.” A low-complexity website with documented control can be the more responsible purchase. A technically elaborate site with unclear ownership can be a worse investment than a modest implementation.

The work a page count conceals

Content is not merely filling a template. A technically valid service page can still misstate eligibility, hours, service area or licensing. A bilingual page is not complete when machine-generated words appear in two languages; claims, contact information, search intent and legal wording need qualified review in each locale. The owner should decide who approves those facts and when a change in one language triggers a counterpart review.

Integrations turn publishing work into operational engineering. A basic enquiry form differs from a routed CRM workflow with spam handling, validation, field mappings, permission checks, exception alerts and test records. For a service owner, the critical deliverable is not the button animation; it is a trustworthy path from an interested visitor to an accountable team. If that path already exists and works, paying to rebuild it may be unnecessary.

Accessibility also belongs in acceptance rather than in a vague final polish allowance. W3C WCAG 2.2 describes testable accessibility criteria. Scope whether the vendor is targeting a stated conformance level, conducting automated checks, manual keyboard/mobile testing, or independent assessment. An automated scanner alone is not a declaration of site-wide WCAG conformance.

For URL changes, Google's site-move guidance describes testing, URL mapping and redirects; it does not guarantee traffic preservation. If URL structures remain stable, document that separately. For multiple locales, Google's localized-page guidance explains reciprocal alternatives. Migration effort is conditional on what is actually moving.

Fill-in 24/36-month TCO worksheet: compare Proposal A and B

Normalize first: choose the same 24- or 36-month horizon (H), stated currency, acceptance scope, quality of support and transferable deliverables. Obtain dated written quotations. Mark every line as included, separately priced, excluded, conditional or unknown. Count annual renewals only when due within H; never treat an unquoted future rate as confirmed.

Committed TCO = unbundled one-time charges + (confirmed monthly charges × H) + scheduled non-monthly renewals within H + known company work hours × actual loaded hourly rate + approved paid change orders + required transfer/exit charges and taxes not already included. Scenario TCO = committed TCO + selected, separately labeled allowances for possible change orders, variable fees or exit/migration. An allowance is not a vendor commitment. Record bundled items once, even if they appear in multiple categories; record any package expiry.

Portability is not a single yes-or-no

“You own your website” is incomplete without identifying what can actually be accessed, copied and operated elsewhere. Ask separately about domain, DNS, text, images and permissions, editable design files, source code, CMS data, localization, form processing, CRM records, analytics history, hosting configuration and licensed plugins. Ownership of content does not imply a particular platform can export the whole functioning application.

For example, Webflow's official export documentation says code export requires an eligible paid Workspace plan and does not include CMS functionality, localized content or operational form handling in the exported site. By contrast, WordPress software is GPL-licensed, but that fact alone does not transfer domain credentials, private data, commercial plugin rights, hosting contracts or the vendor's bespoke design assets. Neither example is a universal argument for or against a platform.

Require a small exit rehearsal before signing: request a sample content export, a list of paid dependencies, administrator roles and the exact artifacts delivered on termination. If an exit would require recreation, treat that as a commercial dependency, not as a surprise for a later owner. A stable vendor relationship can be valuable; undisclosed lock-in is the problem.

Presence, growth or integrated system?

These are decision conditions, not price tiers or branded packages. Choose the lowest tier that covers real obligations; do not fund an integration merely because it could be built.

  1. Presence-level: a stable offer, limited services, approved proof, straightforward contact and low editorial cadence. A reputable template or builder may be economically superior when access, accessibility and operating costs are explicit.
  2. Growth-level: differentiated audiences or services, decision-specific content, localized geography, repeatable content updates and more demanding credibility/measurement. Fund research, IA and content governance before exotic features.
  3. Integrated-system: business-critical appointment logic, CRM/dispatch routing, permissions, multilingual workflows or complex legacy migration. Commission failure handling, observability and change ownership as product work—not merely an attractive front end.

If the business cannot specify what an integration would automate or who handles failures, defer it. If a legal or operational requirement makes the integration indispensable, it belongs in acceptance from day one. For the separate technical platform decision, see the builder-versus-custom analysis; this cost model deliberately does not settle that choice for every firm.

Fictional example: compare two offers

Illustrative scenario only; not a ProAI Expert client, actual quotation or market price. A hypothetical U.S. residential maintenance company operates in two neighboring counties. It has five core services, existing approved photos, one English content owner and a CRM used by dispatch. It wants an updated site and reliable form handoff. It may add a Russian-language service section, but that requirement is not yet approved.

Offer A promises a template-based build, existing copy placement, a basic contact form and launch training for a lower unspecified initial fee. It excludes CRM mapping, copywriting, translation, redirect inventory, ongoing updates and design-source delivery. Offer B includes service architecture workshops, edited English content, CRM handoff validation, migration mapping, accessibility/QA evidence, admin training and a defined support period for a higher unspecified initial fee. Both can be legitimate products. They are not yet equivalents.

Normalize the comparison by requiring both firms to quote the same approved English pages, content responsibilities, verified enquiry delivery, any required redirects, support response and client-held domain/analytics access. Put Russian localization in a separate optional line and require a concrete change order trigger. Ask both vendors for operating and exit terms. Then compare on the same future window without pretending the missing dollar figures are known.

When A wins: internal staff can supply/edit content, a tested standard form safely reaches dispatch, there is no important migration, and the owner accepts platform editing limits. Paying for strategic workshops or a custom CRM pipeline would add little value. When B wins: incorrect enquiry routing threatens operations, legacy URLs require careful movement, services need clearer explanation and the company lacks internal editorial/QA capacity. The value is documented responsibility and reduced uncertainty—not a guaranteed conversion uplift.

Run a pre-contract test: can each supplier show who fixes a failed submission, who owns the domain and how an editor publishes a service-area change? An answer supported by a deliverable matters more than the agency's adjectives.

A scoping brief to send vendors

Copy this into an enquiry or RFP. Request a written response against each line, with included / excluded / conditional, cost basis, milestones, responsible owner and acceptance evidence. Blank responses should remain open risks, not silently treated as included.

  1. Business: priority services, customer segments, actual cities/counties served, exclusions and target contact routes.
  2. Inventory: current site and domain, content/pages to retain, existing URLs, analytics/search access, assets and licenses.
  3. Languages: launch locales, who authors/approves each, counterpart changes and ongoing localization responsibility.
  4. Content and evidence: who interviews experts, writes, edits, approves claims, secures photo rights and enters content.
  5. Architecture and UX: required service paths, navigation, mobile states, interactions, forms and error behavior.
  6. Integrations: CRM, scheduling, email delivery, data fields, permissions, logging, fallback routes and third-party charges.
  7. Migration and discovery: stable versus changed URLs, mapping/redirect ownership, metadata, locale links, launch monitoring.
  8. Accessibility and verification: target standard, browser/device matrix, manual tests, issue severity and retest responsibilities.
  9. Schedule and dependencies: realistic launch criteria, client approvals, blocked-work policy and change-control mechanism.
  10. Operations: hosting, licenses, renewal risk, backups, security, incident response, support windows and optional retainer.
  11. Handover and exit: client-controlled domain/DNS, source and editable files, credentials, data exports, admin training, termination obligations.
  12. Commercial comparison: one-time items, recurring subscriptions, separately quoted optional work, taxes/fees if relevant, payment milestones and final acceptance.

Do not send customer credentials or private CRM exports with an initial RFP. Supply sensitive material through an agreed controlled process only after legitimate access and confidentiality terms are in place.

What the owner can control

Owner-controlled scope: lock service priorities, designate a decision maker, prepare accurate copy and evidence, obtain permissions, set a defined revision limit, decide whether both languages launch together, and approve acceptance tests early. These actions can reduce avoidable rework but are not guaranteed cost savings. If internal people supply content, account for their time rather than calling the work free.

Externally constrained exposure: platform subscriptions, domain and hosting renewals, licensed components, usage-based CRM/API fees, tax treatment, third-party policy changes and vendor availability. Verify these against current supplier contracts and price schedules on the date of quotation. The vendor should disclose which costs may change and who monitors them.

Do not optimize away essentials: reliable enquiry delivery, ownership access, backups, truthful service claims, accessible interactions, migration safeguards or handover evidence. When budgets are constrained, defer secondary animations, low-priority templates or noncritical integrations instead of disguising operating risk as a discount.

Choose and commission responsibly

A responsible decision has four outputs: a completed 24/36-month TCO comparison with explicit unknowns, a signed scope-and-exclusions sheet, a lifecycle-and-exit sheet, and a testable acceptance plan. If either vendor cannot produce these, ask for a clarification before comparing totals. A transparent simple build can be the correct decision; an expensive but undefined scope is not inherently “premium.”
